Notes on the evidence

1 The Abbasid Revolution (747–750) overthrew the Umayyads in the name of the Prophet’s uncle al-Abbas, but its armies were raised and led overwhelmingly by Iranian (Khorasani) supporters under the general Abu Muslim — himself executed by Al-Mansur in 755 once his influence became a threat. Iran’s support was decisive in bringing the dynasty to power even before any Abbasid caliph set foot there as more than a distant sovereign.

2 Al-Amin and Al-Ma’mun’s rivalry (the “Fourth Fitna,” 809–813) pitted a younger son of two Abbasid parents — Zubaida was herself a granddaughter of Al-Mansur — against an older half-brother born to a Persian concubine. Al-Ma’mun’s eventual victory, and his reliance on Khorasani troops and administrators, marked a further deepening of Iranian influence at the highest levels of the caliphate.

3 The decade after Al-Mutawakkil’s murder in 861 saw four caliphs killed or deposed in rapid succession by the Turkic slave-soldier (ghilman) regiments Al-Mu’tasim had installed at the new capital of Samarra — a period Muslim historians call the “Anarchy at Samarra,” when military commanders, not the caliphs, held real power.

4 The court reasserted some order after 870, though effective control of Iran and Iraq increasingly passed to regional dynasties — the Saffarids, Samanids and others — who paid the caliphs only nominal homage.

5 From 945 the Shi’a Iranian Buyid dynasty controlled Baghdad and reduced the caliphs to ceremonial, religious figureheads; the Sunni Seljuk Turks displaced the Buyids in 1055 but kept the caliphate in the same subordinate role for another century.

6 Seljuk power collapsed in the 12th century, letting a handful of late caliphs — especially An-Nasir — rebuild real authority over Iraq itself, though never again over Iran at large. Al-Musta’sim was executed when Hulagu Khan’s Mongol army sacked Baghdad in 1258, ending the caliphate there; a surviving relative was installed as a ceremonial “shadow caliph” under Mamluk protection in Cairo, a line that lasted until the Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517.

Most Abbasid caliphs after the ninth century were born to enslaved concubines (umm walad) rather than free wives; some of these women, such as Shaghab and Zumurrud Khatun, wielded major political power as regents, while many others are recorded by name alone or not at all.
