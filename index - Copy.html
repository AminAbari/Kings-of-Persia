The Kings of Kings

Pre-Islamic Iran

559 BCE – 651 CE

From Cyrus the Great’s unification of the Persians and Medes to the Arab conquest that ended Sasanian rule, this is the age of the shahanshah — nearly twelve centuries in which native Iranian and foreign dynasties alike claimed the title King of Kings.

First Persian Empire

The Achaemenid Dynasty

559 – 330 BCE 229 years

Founded by Cyrus the Great and expanded by Darius I and Xerxes, the Achaemenids ruled the largest empire the ancient world had yet seen, from the Aegean to the Indus.

Carved from Alexander’s conquests by his general Seleucus Nicator, the Seleucids ruled Persia, Mesopotamia and Syria as a Greco-Iranian successor kingdom for over two centuries.

From the Arab Conquest to the Eve of the Safavids

Arrival of Islam: Caliphates, Sultanates & Turko-Mongol Rule

651 – 1501 CE

From the Arab conquest to the eve of the Safavids, Iran was ruled in turn by Arab caliphs, native Persian regional dynasties, and successive waves of Turkic and Mongol conquerors — 850 years in which the Persian language and culture endured, and often flourished, under almost continuous foreign rule.

The Caliphates

632 – 1258 CE

Arab caliphs ruled Iran from the conquest onward — the Rashidun, then the Umayyads of Damascus and the Abbasids of Baghdad. The Abbasid caliphate outlasted every other dynasty in this section, surviving as a figurehead long after real power had passed to others, until the Mongols ended it in 1258.

The Arab Conquest

Rashidun Caliphate

632 – 661 CE 29 years

Muhammad’s first four successors extended Arab rule from Arabia into Persia, ending four centuries of Sasanian kingship within a single generation.

Umayyad Caliphate

Ruling from Damascus, the Umayyads turned the caliphate into a hereditary Arab monarchy stretching from Spain to the edge of India.

The Caliphate of Baghdad

Abbasid Caliphate

750 – 1258 CE 508 years

Brought to power by Iranian revolutionaries, the Abbasids ruled — and later merely reigned over — the Islamic world from Baghdad for five centuries, until the Mongols ended it in 1258.

Regional Dynasties & Turkic Sultanates

819 – 1231 CE

As Abbasid power weakened, native Persian dynasties — the Samanids, Tahirids, Saffarids, Ziyarids and Buyids — took control of Iran’s provinces. They were followed by Turkic sultanates: the Ghaznavids, the Great Seljuks and the Khwarazmshahs.

Revival of Persian Culture

Samanid Empire

819 – 999 CE 180 years

Ruling from Bukhara, the Samanids revived New Persian as a literary language and patronized the poets whose work fed directly into Ferdowsi’s Shahnameh.

Khwarazmian Empire

The Mongol Age & Its Successors

1256 – 1507 CE

The Mongol invasions destroyed the Khwarazmian Empire and, in 1258, the Abbasid caliphate. After the Ilkhanate collapsed, Persianate, Turkmen and Teymourian successor states divided Iran among themselves until the Safavids reunited it.

The Mongol Ilkhans

Ilkhanate

1256 – 1335 CE 79 years

Descendants of Genghis Khan who sacked Baghdad and ended the Abbasid caliphate, before converting to Islam and patronizing Persian art and historiography.

The National Dynasties

Early Modern & Modern Iran

1501 – 1979 CE

From the Safavid founding of a unified, explicitly Shi’a Iranian state to the Pahlavi dynasty’s fall in 1979, this is the story of Iran’s re-emergence as a coherent nation — and its collision with European empire, oil and revolution.

Unifier of Modern Iran

The Safavid Dynasty

1501 – 1736 CE 235 years

Ismail I united Iran under Twelver Shi’ism for the first time, founding a dynasty whose court at Isfahan became a center of Persian art and architecture.
