Notes on the evidence

1 The Saffarids were founded and led entirely by brothers and descendants of a single family from Sistan, in Iran’s southeast, who never held more than nominal caliphal recognition and ruled by conquest first, legitimacy second.

2 Ya’qub ibn al-Layth began as a saffar — a coppersmith — before turning soldier and carving out a state stretching from Sistan through Khorasan into Fars, marching as far as the outskirts of Baghdad in 876 before being defeated. His rise from an artisan background to founding a ruling dynasty is almost unique among the medieval Iranian and Islamic states covered on this site. He is said to have told the caliph’s envoys, who offered peace, that he would share his soldiers’ fate of bread and the sword rather than accept empty titles.

3 Amr ibn al-Layth was captured in battle at Balkh in 900 by Ismail ibn Ahmad, the Samanid ruler, ending Saffarid control of Khorasan and opening the way for Samanid dominance of the east. The rulers listed after Amr held only Sistan itself, amid rapid, contested succession, before falling fully under Samanid overlordship.

A much-reduced Saffarid line continued as local, tributary rulers of Sistan alone for centuries afterward — under Samanid, then Ghaznavid, then Ghurid suzerainty — with a chronology of minor amirs too fragmentary to reconstruct reliably here, until Mongol conquest finally extinguished it in the 13th century.
