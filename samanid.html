Notes on the evidence

1 The Samanids descended from Saman Khuda, a Zoroastrian noble of Balkh who converted to Islam; his descendants served as Abbasid and Tahirid governors before Ahmad ibn Asad’s sons consolidated the family’s scattered provincial appointments into a single hereditary state.

2 Ismail ibn Ahmad’s capture of the Saffarid ruler Amr ibn al-Layth at Balkh in 900 is usually treated as the true founding moment of full Samanid sovereignty over Khorasan and Transoxiana. He remains a revered national figure in Iran, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan today.

3 The Samanid court at Bukhara is credited above all as the great patron of the revival of New Persian as a literary language, home to poets such as Rudaki — often called the father of Persian poetry — and Daqiqi, whose unfinished verse epic was later completed by Ferdowsi as the Shahnameh. This cultural legacy arguably outlasted the dynasty’s political power by centuries.

4 Turkic slave-soldier commanders in Samanid service, especially Alptigin and his son-in-law and successor Sebuktegin at Ghazna, steadily gained autonomy through the later 10th century, laying the foundation for the Ghaznavid Empire that would help bring down Samanid rule.

5 Bukhara fell to the Qarakhanid Turks in 999, ending the main Samanid line; a prince, Ismail Muntasir, continued resistance in Khorasan and Transoxiana until his death around 1005.
