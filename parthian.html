Notes on the evidence

1 Every Arsacid king ruled under the single throne-name “Arsaces,” recorded on coinage simply as ΑΡΣΑΚΗΣ (“Arsaces”) in Greek, the language of Parthian court coin legends. The individual names used in this table — Mithridates, Phraates, Vologases and the rest — are the personal names by which ancient historians and modern scholars distinguish one king from another; they rarely appear on the coins themselves. Arsaces I’s own origins are contested: Arrian’s Parthica calls him chief of the Parni, a nomadic Iranian tribe of the steppe, while later Arsacid court tradition claimed descent from the Achaemenids. His Parthian name, Aršak, survives in New Persian as “Ashk,” the root of Ashkanian (اشکانیان), the modern Persian name for the whole dynasty.

2 The years 87–80 BCE are among the worst-attested in Parthian history. A king “Orodes I” is known chiefly from coinage, and the sequence and dates of rulers in this decade remain genuinely debated among specialists.

3 Sinatruces is said by the satirist Lucian to have been placed on the throne in old age after decades among the Sacaraucae, a Saka nomadic people of Central Asia. His parentage and exact relationship to the ruling Arsacid line are unknown.

4 Orodes II had his brother and co-heir Mithridates III (r. c. 57–54 BCE, omitted above as a short-lived rival) deposed and executed after seizing sole power. He is best known for annihilating a Roman invasion under Crassus at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BCE.

5 Musa was an Italian slave woman sent to Phraates IV as a gift by the Roman emperor Augustus. She became his queen, was widely suspected of poisoning him, and then married their own son Phraates V and ruled jointly with him — a scandal recorded by Josephus.

6 Vonones I had been sent to Rome as a boy hostage. His Roman manners made him unpopular on the Parthian throne, and he was quickly deposed in favor of Artabanus II.

Parthian chronology rests heavily on coin legends and Babylonian astronomical diaries rather than a continuous royal annal, and competing modern reconstructions of the 1st century BCE–1st century CE sequence can differ by several years. Rival co-kings ruled simultaneously over different provinces for much of the empire’s final two centuries; only the better-attested claimants are listed here. The Persian forms given here are New Persian renderings of the reconstructed Parthian names — several, such as Farhad, Mehrdad, Godarz and Khosrow, survive as personal names in Iran to this day.
