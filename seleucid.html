Notes on the evidence

1 Laodice IV was a daughter of Antiochus III who married, in turn, her brothers Seleucus IV and then Antiochus IV — making Antiochus V's mother also his father's sister, a pattern of sibling marriage recurring in the dynasty's final generations.

2 Alexander Balas's claim to be a son of Antiochus IV Epiphanes is reported by 1 Maccabees and Diodorus, but ancient historians such as Justin/Trogus regarded it as a fiction of convenience for a low-born pretender raised up by Pergamon, Egypt and Rome to unseat Demetrius I.

3 Cleopatra Thea, daughter of Ptolemy VI of Egypt, married three Seleucid kings in succession — Alexander Balas, Demetrius II, and Antiochus VII — and was mother to three more. She later ruled in her own name and jointly with her sons, an unusual span of direct female sovereignty for the Hellenistic world.

4 Diodotus Tryphon was a Seleucid official from Apamea, not of royal blood. He rose as regent for the child-king Antiochus VI before deposing and reportedly murdering him and reigning independently.

5 From 96 BCE the throne fractured among rival cousins and half-brothers, several ruling simultaneously over shrinking, overlapping territories. Their chronology is reconstructed largely from coinage and remains genuinely disputed among historians; the dates above should be read as approximate.

6 Tigranes II of Armenia was not of Seleucid blood. He annexed a exhausted, war-torn Syria as a foreign conqueror during the interregnum, ruling it until driven out by Rome — after which the last Seleucid claimants briefly returned before Pompey ended the dynasty.

7 Unlike the Achaemenids, Parthians and Sasanians, the Seleucids were a Greco-Macedonian dynasty with no native Iranian throne names of their own — see the shaded “foreign rule” entry on the home page. The Persian-script forms given here are simply the standard modern Persian transliterations of each king's Greek name, as used in Persian-language scholarship on the “Seleukiyan” (سلوکیان) period, not a native Iranian title.

The principal ancient sources are Appian's Syriaca, Justin's epitome of Pompeius Trogus, Diodorus Siculus, Josephus and 1–2 Maccabees, supplemented heavily by royal coinage — especially for the fragmented final decades, where literary sources largely fail.
